1Goals & what is different here

The engine prices FX vanillas and first-generation exotics (barriers, touches, digitals) and maintains live portfolio risk, serving two consumers with opposite demands: an e-trading gateway that must respond to RFQs and refresh streamed quotes within milliseconds of a spot tick, and a desk risk view that must be complete, smile-aware, and consistent rather than merely fast. It inherits the multi-curve framework's machinery — dependency graph, epochs, journals, tiered scheduling, the three rings — and this document assumes that machinery rather than restating it. What deserves attention is where FX options genuinely differ from curve-building:

Three input cadences, three orders of magnitude apart. Spot ticks tens of times per second per pair. Forward points and vol quotes (ATM, risk reversals, butterflies by tenor) tick in seconds to minutes. Rates curves arrive as epochs from the multi-curve system on its own cadence. A design that recalibrates everything on every spot tick wastes three orders of magnitude; one that waits for full recalibration to refresh quotes is unquotable. The central structural idea of this engine — the two-speed surface (§4) — falls directly out of this cadence mismatch.

The calibrated object is a surface, not a curve. Vol surfaces are quoted in delta space (ATM plus 10Δ/25Δ risk reversals and butterflies), constructed per pair with pair-specific conventions, and must satisfy no-arbitrage conditions in two dimensions (strike convexity, calendar monotonicity). Construction methodology is a governed model choice, not a detail.

Pricing is model-dependent, and the engine must not care which model. A vanilla prices off closed-form with a smile lookup in microseconds; a barrier prices off a Vanna-Volga adjustment in tens of microseconds or an SLV PDE in milliseconds, and the desk's view of which is right will evolve. Per our configuration principles, models are named, versioned strategies behind a uniform contract; the engine orchestrates, models compute, and a governed selection matrix binds instrument × purpose to model (§5).

Risk must tick without repricing the world. A 10k-position book cannot fully reprice on every spot tick, and does not need to: cached Greeks plus a Taylor update track risk between full revaluations, with an explicit error budget forcing full revaluation when drift accumulates (§6).

2System overview

Figure 1 — Component overview. The multi-curve framework is an upstream peer, not a subsystem: its curve-set epochs enter the surface service (forwards, discounting) and the pricing core exactly as market data. Fills from e-trading flow into the trade journal and appear in risk within one cycle.

The flow in one sentence per layer: the market data layer does for spot, forwards, and vol quotes what its curve-side counterpart does for swap quotes — normalize, validate, composite, conflate, journal. The vol surface service is the analogue of the curve engine: it owns the surface dependency graph, calibrates on the slow cadence, re-anchors on the fast one, and publishes immutable surface epochs. The pricing core is a stateless function library behind uniform contracts — given an instrument and a coherent market slice, return PV and Greeks, via whichever named model the selection matrix binds. The risk engine folds the trade journal into positions and maintains live portfolio Greeks by the two-speed discipline. The e-trading gateway turns prices into quotes under Ring-3 trader controls.

3FX market data layer

Mechanically this layer inherits everything from the curve system — canonical quote model, validation, composites, last-value cache with conflation as the load-shedding boundary, append-only journal. What is FX-specific is the semantic validation, because FX vol quotes are meaningless without their conventions. Every vol quote carries: delta convention (spot vs forward delta; premium-adjusted for pairs where premium is paid in foreign currency, e.g. USDJPY), ATM convention (delta-neutral straddle vs at-the-forward), and quote form (RR/BF as smile strategies vs outright vols). The validator refuses quotes whose conventions disagree with the pair's configured definitions — a 25Δ RR interpreted under the wrong delta convention produces a subtly wrong smile, which is worse than no smile.

Beyond per-quote checks, the layer runs cross-field coherence checks that only exist in FX: covered interest parity residuals between forward points and the rates curves (persistent drift means the FX basis is moving — a signal to surface and curve builders both, and a monitored quantity in its own right), spot/forward staleness skew (fresh spot with stale forwards is a common and dangerous feed failure), and triangulation residuals on crosses (EURUSD × USDJPY vs quoted EURJPY spot). Trader marks — vol overrides at specific pillars, RR/BF adjustments in illiquid pairs — are Ring-3 operational controls: entitled, audited, auto-expiring, journaled as market events, flagged downstream.

4The volatility surface service

Construction as a strategy pipeline

Surface construction is a per-pair pipeline of named strategies, each a Ring-1 behavior selected by Ring-2 configuration: quote preparation (strategy-space to strike-space conversion under the pair's conventions), smile fit per pillar tenor (named strategies: parametric SABR-per-tenor, polynomial-in-delta, SVI-in-strike, Vanna-Volga-implied), temporal interpolation (total variance in weighted vol time — the event calendar assigns weights to dates so that weekends, holidays, and known event days like central-bank meetings and payrolls carry their empirically distinct share of variance), and extrapolation policy in both wings and long tenors. The pipeline emits a SurfaceSnapshot: fitted parameters per pillar, the interpolation spec, conventions, and full lineage — quote journal positions, curve-set epoch used for forwards and discounting, config version, strategy names and versions.

Arbitrage gates

Between calibration and publication sit the no-arbitrage checks: strike-space convexity (butterfly arbitrage) per tenor, calendar monotonicity of total variance along constant-moneyness lines, and wing behavior bounds. Like the curve system's quality gates, failure never publishes silently wrong: the surface republishes last-good with a degraded flag, per pillar where the failure is local (one bad butterfly quote should degrade one tenor's smile, not the pair).

The two-speed surface

The cadence mismatch of §1 is resolved by separating what a spot tick means from what a vol tick means. FX smiles are quoted and risk-managed in delta space, which makes the market's own convention approximately sticky-delta: when spot moves and vol quotes have not, the desk-standard presumption is that the smile rides with the new forward. So the fast path treats the calibrated surface as a function of the forward: on a spot or forward-point tick, the surface service republishes a re-anchored view — same fitted smile parameters, new forward — in well under a millisecond, without touching the calibrator. The slow path recalibrates fully when vol quotes, curves, or configuration change, or when spot has drifted beyond a configured band from the last calibration anchor (at which point the sticky-delta presumption itself is stale). Re-anchored views carry both the surface epoch of their parent calibration and a fast-path sub-version, so every consumer knows exactly which regime a number came from.

The two-speed structure is the curve system's tier pattern, transposed: re-anchoring is the fast tier (cheap, provably consistent with the calibrated object, refreshed per spot tick), recalibration is the comprehensive tier (the full solve, on the cadence of the inputs that actually justify it). And the same invariant applies: the fast path is a latency optimization, never a second opinion — re-anchor and recalibrate must agree when inputs are quiet, which is monitored, not assumed.
Figure 3 — The two-speed loop. The fast path never calibrates and the slow path never blocks quoting: re-anchored views keep streaming between calibrations, and every number is stamped with the regime it came from.

Cross pairs

Cross surfaces (EURJPY from EURUSD and USDJPY) are graph nodes like any other: where the cross is liquidly quoted, it calibrates from its own quotes with triangulation consistency monitored; where it is not, it is implied through the majors with a correlation input — itself a marked, governed quantity — via a named implication strategy. The dependency graph handles both cases naturally, and a tick in USDJPY vol dirties every cross that implicates it, exactly as a curve tick dirties dependent curves.

Figure 2 — Surface dependency graph for a major/cross family. Curve-set epochs from the multi-curve framework enter as inputs wherever forwards and discounting are needed. The EURJPY surface blends its own sparse quotes with implication through the majors; a USDJPY vol tick dirties USDJPY and EURJPY surfaces, and nothing else.

5The pricing core

Contracts, not models

The core defines three contracts and is deliberately ignorant of everything else. An Instrument is a typed description of the trade (vanilla, barrier variant, touch, digital) with its economic terms — no pricing logic. A MarketSlice is a coherent, immutable bundle of everything a model may read: surface epoch (or re-anchored view), curve-set epoch, spot, forwards — assembled by the coherence layer (§8), never by models. A PricingModel is a named, versioned Ring-1 strategy implementing price(instrument, slice, request) → PricingResult, where the request states which outputs are needed (PV only for a quote; full Greeks for risk) so models can skip unneeded work.

The PricingResult carries PV, Greeks, and — importantly — metadata about how each Greek was computed: analytic, bumped (with bump spec), or adjoint. The Greeks contract standardizes FX conventions once, centrally: delta as spot or forward delta, premium-adjusted where the pair's convention says so; vega expressed against the surface's parameterization; vanna and volga for the first-gen book; theta with a stated carry convention. Models declare capabilities; the engine never guesses.

The model selection matrix

Which model prices which instrument for which purpose is a governed Ring-2 configuration — a matrix over (instrument class × purpose), where purpose distinguishes screen/stream (speed-critical), risk (consistency-critical), and official (governance-critical). A representative desk setup:

The matrix is data; the models it names are code. Adding SLV later — calibration farm, PDE pricers — changes the registry and the matrix, not the engine: this is precisely what "model-agnostic" buys, and why it is the architecture rather than a slogan. Where stream and official models differ for the same instrument, the difference is a monitored quantity (the model basis), not a surprise discovered at EOD.

6The risk engine

Portfolio state

Positions are a fold over the trade journal — booked, amended, exercised, expired — so intraday state is reproducible and an e-trading fill appears in risk within one cycle of hitting the journal. Positions bucket by pair, then by whatever the desk's risk views need; the engine maintains per-position cached results and per-bucket aggregates.

Two-speed risk

The surface's two-speed structure extends to the book. On the fast path, a spot tick updates portfolio risk through cached Greeks: ΔPV ≈ delta·dS + ½·gamma·dS² + vega·dσ(re-anchor), applied per position and aggregated — microseconds for the book, no model calls. Each position tracks an error budget: accumulated |dS| and dσ since its last full revaluation, weighted by its own gamma, vanna, and volga (the terms that make Taylor drift). Breaching the budget, crossing near a barrier level, or approaching expiry forces the position into the next revaluation wave — the slow path, which fully reprices dirty positions against the latest calibrated surface epoch on a rolling cadence, prioritized by staleness × materiality. Barriers near their trigger get special treatment: their budgets tighten as spot approaches the level, because Taylor expansion is precisely wrong where the payoff kinks.

Smile-bucket vega and quote-space risk

The Jacobian machinery transfers unchanged: the surface calibrator publishes ∂(surface parameters)/∂(vol quotes) at each calibration, so node-space vega maps to the trader's native ladder — ATM vega, RR sensitivity, BF sensitivity per tenor — with a matrix multiply. Chained with the curve system's Jacobians, the same trade shows its rates risk in curve-quote space. One mechanism, both asset classes, no bump-and-recalibrate in the live path — with the batch bump service retained as the exact cross-check, exactly as on the curve side.

Scenarios

The classic FX risk views — spot ladder × vol shift matrices per pair, barrier-level proximity reports, event-date vega — run as batch products of the same pricing contracts on the comprehensive cadence, using frozen coherent slices. Nothing about them is architecturally special; they are consumers of the core, which is the point.

7The e-trading path

The gateway holds the latest re-anchored surface view per pair and answers two shapes of demand. RFQ: price the requested instrument off the current slice via the stream-purpose model, apply the spread engine, respond — total budget ~1 ms for vanillas, of which pricing is microseconds. Streaming: maintain quote grids (tenor × delta or explicit strikes) per pair, refreshed by surface re-anchor on every spot tick and by recalibration when vol epochs land; grid points are repriced incrementally, dirty-flagged exactly like everything else in this architecture.

The spread engine is where Ring 3 lives on this path: base spreads by pair/tenor/size, skew by inventory (leaning the quote against accumulating position), widen-on-uncertainty rules bound to the quality flags flowing through the system — a degraded surface, a stale forward, a breached tier-agreement check all mechanically widen or pull quotes. Trader fingertip controls: per-pair widen/pull, size caps, kill switches — entitled, audited, auto-expiring, journaled. The gateway also enforces the coherence rule that matters most commercially: it never quotes off a mixed slice; if the surface epoch and curve epoch it holds are inconsistent (§8), it quotes off the last coherent pair or widens, by configuration.

8Coherence & multi-curve integration

The engine consumes the curve system's outputs as epochs and produces its own — surfaces, risk — as epochs. Coherence across the federation is handled by the MarketSlice: an immutable binding of {spot version, forward version, curve-set epoch, surface epoch or re-anchored sub-version} assembled centrally with declared staleness tolerances between components. The rule of the curve system — consumers price against an epoch, never against a mixture — generalizes to: consumers price against a slice. Slices are cheap (references, not copies), constructed at each fast-path tick and at each slow-path epoch, and stamped on every price and risk number the system emits.

Cross-system consistency is monitored where the two systems' views can drift: forwards implied by curve-set epochs versus quoted forward points (the FX basis, again), and surface calibrations pinned to a curve epoch that has since moved (surfaces record their curve lineage; a curve epoch change marks dependent surfaces dirty through the same graph). Replay works across the federation because every store is a journal and every derived object records its inputs' versions: reproducing 14:32:07 means fetching the slice that was current then and re-running deterministic code — the same discipline, now spanning two systems.

10Key design decisions

D1The two-speed surface: re-anchor on spot, recalibrate on vol

Encodes the market's own sticky-delta presumption as architecture, matching compute cadence to input cadence. Alternatives — recalibrate per spot tick (wasteful by 100×, and noisy), or serve stale surfaces between calibrations (unquotable) — both lose. Guardrails: the drift band that forces recalibration when spot leaves the anchor's neighborhood, and continuous agreement monitoring between the two paths.

D2Model-agnostic pricing behind named-strategy contracts

The engine owns orchestration, coherence, and conventions; models own math. The selection matrix (Ring 2) binds instrument × purpose to model, so upgrading barriers from Vanna-Volga to SLV is a registry entry, a matrix change, and a calibration farm — not an engine rewrite. This is the configuration papers' structure/behavior boundary applied to quant models, where it matters most: model choice is precisely the value that must be governed (model validation signs the matrix), while model code is precisely the behavior that must not leak into config.

D3Taylor-tracked risk with an explicit error budget

Full reprice per tick is unaffordable; pure cached Greeks silently rot. The error budget makes the approximation's validity a first-class, monitored quantity, with per-position tightening where Taylor is least trustworthy (barriers near trigger, short-dated near ATM). The alternative of a fixed reval frequency spends compute where nothing moved and starves positions that moved a lot.

D4Risk in the trader's quote space via calibration Jacobians

ATM/RR/BF vega ladders and curve-quote rates risk from one mechanism — IFT Jacobians published with each calibrated object, chained across the federation — with batch bump-and-revalue as the standing cross-check. Identical to the curve system's D7, deliberately: one risk-transformation idiom across asset classes.

D5Federation by epochs and slices, not shared state

The multi-curve system remains an autonomous peer; integration is subscription to its epochs plus lineage recording, and coherence is the slice abstraction with declared staleness tolerances. The alternative — one merged engine — couples release cycles, failure domains, and teams for no computational gain; the graph already spans systems logically without sharing a process.

D6Quality flags drive quoting behavior mechanically

Degraded surfaces, stale forwards, and tier disagreements widen or pull quotes through configured rules rather than human vigilance — the "degrade loudly" principle extended to its commercial endpoint. The alternative, trusting operators to notice a flag before the auto-quoter streams off a bad surface, has a well-documented failure history in this industry.

12Suggested build order

Phase 1 — the deterministic core, again first. Pair definitions and conventions engine, quote model and journal, one smile strategy end-to-end (say SABR-per-tenor), arbitrage gates, surface epochs with lineage, the pricing contracts with the closed-form vanilla model, replay harness spanning FX and (recorded) curve epochs. Prove that a recorded day replays bit-identically before anything ticks.

Phase 2 — the two-speed loop. Live feeds for two or three majors, conflation, re-anchoring path, streamed grids, RFQ path for vanillas with the spread engine and Ring-3 controls, surface viewer. A desk can stream vanilla prices at the end of this phase.

Phase 3 — risk and the first-gen book. Trade-journal fold, cached-Greek fast path with error budgets, revaluation waves, Vanna-Volga models for barriers/touches/digitals, smile-bucket vega via Jacobians, spot/vol matrices, crosses with implication.

Phase 4 — hardening and depth. Quality-gate-driven quoting rules, failover (deterministic twin, as the curve system), official marks workflow, model basis monitoring, and — when the desk's book justifies it — the SLV calibration farm and PDE pricers slotting into the existing registry and matrix, which by then is a routine registry addition rather than a project.

The ordering principle is unchanged from the curve system: determinism first, latency second, breadth third, resilience last — and the fact that the principle transfers unchanged is itself the argument for having built the first system the way we did.
